Chapter 1

Derivatives Revisited

Before training multi-layer neural networks, we must build absolute mastery of the instantaneous rate of change. We explore physical intuition, first-order linear approximations, and the trade-offs between exact analytical derivatives and numerical finite differences.

🔗
Where you'll use this in CS: Learning rate scheduling in PyTorch/TensorFlow, finite-difference gradient checking for custom CUDA kernels (torch.autograd.gradcheck), and numerical physics simulation in 3D game engines (Verlet integration).
🎯 Learning Objectives
  • Compute exact analytical derivatives of standard machine learning loss and activation functions (Sigmoid, GELU, Softplus).
  • Construct first-order linear approximations and quantify their local truncation errors.
  • Evaluate forward vs symmetric central difference approximations and explain why floating-point cancellation limits step size $h$.
💡 3. Intuition First

Imagine driving down the Mumbai-Pune Expressway. If you drive 100 kilometres in one hour, your average speed is $100\text{ km/h}$. But at any specific moment, your speedometer may have read $125\text{ km/h}$ or $0\text{ km/h}$ at a toll booth.

The derivative is simply that instantaneous speedometer reading. Graphically, if you zoom into any smooth non-linear curve at a microscopic level, the curve straightens out until it is indistinguishable from a flat straight line. That local line is the tangent line, and its slope is the derivative.

📐 4. Formal Theory

Definition (The Derivative): Let $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ be defined on an open interval containing $x$. The derivative $f'(x)$ is:

$$f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}$$

First-Order Linear Approximation (Taylor Expansion):

$$f(x + \Delta x) \approx f(x) + f'(x) \Delta x, \quad \text{with error } E(\Delta x) = \mathcal{O}(\Delta x^2)$$

Numerical Differentiation Formulas:

  • Forward Difference: $\frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} = f'(x) + \frac{h}{2}f''(x) + \mathcal{O}(h^2)$   $\implies$ Truncation error is $\mathcal{O}(h)$.
  • Central Difference: $\frac{f(x+h) - f(x-h)}{2h} = f'(x) + \frac{h^2}{6}f'''(x) + \mathcal{O}(h^4)$   $\implies$ Truncation error is $\mathcal{O}(h^2)$.

The Floating-Point Trade-off: Why can't we just set $h = 10^{-25}$? In 64-bit IEEE 754 arithmetic, machine precision is $\epsilon_{\text{mach}} \approx 10^{-16}$. When $h < \epsilon_{\text{mach}}$, $x+h$ and $x$ round to identical bits, causing $(f(x+h)-f(x))$ to suffer catastrophic cancellation to zero! Total error is minimized when truncation error equals roundoff error, yielding optimal step $h^* \approx \sqrt{\epsilon_{\text{mach}}} \approx 10^{-8}$.

✍️ 5. Worked Examples (Increasing Difficulty)
Example 1.1 (Basic Linear Approximation):
Let $f(x) = x^3 - 4x + 1$. Compute $f'(2)$ and approximate $f(2.05)$ using the tangent line.
1. Exact derivative: $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 4 \implies f'(2) = 3(4) - 4 = 8$.
2. Base value: $f(2) = 2^3 - 4(2) + 1 = 8 - 8 + 1 = 1$.
3. Tangent line approximation: $f(2.05) \approx f(2) + f'(2)(0.05) = 1 + 8(0.05) = 1 + 0.40 = 1.40$.
4. True value: $f(2.05) = (2.05)^3 - 4(2.05) + 1 = 8.615125 - 8.20 + 1 = 1.415125$.
5. Error: $|1.415125 - 1.40| = 0.015125 \approx \frac{1}{2} f''(2) (0.05)^2 = 3(2)(0.0025) = 0.0150$ ✓.
Example 1.2 (Sigmoid Activation Function):
Prove that for the logistic sigmoid $\sigma(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}$, the derivative satisfies $\sigma'(x) = \sigma(x)(1 - \sigma(x))$, and evaluate it at $x = 0$ and $x = 2$.
1. Rewrite as $\sigma(x) = (1 + e^{-x})^{-1}$.
2. By the chain rule: $\sigma'(x) = -(1 + e^{-x})^{-2} \cdot (-e^{-x}) = \frac{e^{-x}}{(1 + e^{-x})^2}$.
3. Algebraic factoring: $\frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}} \cdot \frac{e^{-x}}{1 + e^{-x}} = \sigma(x) \cdot \left(\frac{1 + e^{-x} - 1}{1 + e^{-x}}\right) = \sigma(x)(1 - \sigma(x))$ (Q.E.D.).
4. At $x = 0$: $\sigma(0) = \frac{1}{2} \implies \sigma'(0) = 0.5(1 - 0.5) = 0.25$.
5. At $x = 2$: $\sigma(2) \approx 0.8808 \implies \sigma'(2) = 0.8808(1 - 0.8808) = 0.8808(0.1192) \approx 0.1050$.
Example 1.3 (Forward vs Central Difference Numerical Error):
For $f(x) = \ln(x)$ at $x = 1$, compute the forward and central difference approximations with $h = 0.1$.
1. Exact derivative: $f'(x) = \frac{1}{x} \implies f'(1) = 1.000000$.
2. $f(1) = 0$, $f(1 + 0.1) = \ln(1.1) \approx 0.095310$, $f(1 - 0.1) = \ln(0.9) \approx -0.105361$.
3. Forward difference: $\frac{0.095310 - 0}{0.1} = 0.953102 \implies \text{Error} = 0.046898 \approx \frac{h}{2} = 0.05$.
4. Central difference: $\frac{0.095310 - (-0.105361)}{0.2} = \frac{0.200671}{0.2} = 1.003353 \implies \text{Error} = 0.003353 \approx \frac{h^2}{6} = 0.00167$.
5. Notice that central difference is over **$14\times$ more accurate** at the exact same step size!

⚠️ Common Student Mistakes
1. Choosing $h = 10^{-20}$ expecting zero truncation error
Why it fails: Due to floating-point rounding limits ($\epsilon_{\text{mach}} \approx 10^{-16}$), $(x+h)$ rounds to $x$ and the numerator evaluates to exactly $0.0$, wiping out the gradient. Always use $h \approx 10^{-5}$ to $10^{-8}$.
2. Assuming derivatives always exist everywhere
Why it fails: Activations like $\text{ReLU}(x) = \max(0, x)$ have no classical derivative at $x = 0$. Frameworks use subgradients (conventionally setting $g'(0) = 0$).
3. Forgetting the $2h$ denominator in central difference
Why it fails: The distance between $x+h$ and $x-h$ is $2h$, not $h$. Dividing by $h$ doubles the slope estimate.
📌 Key Takeaways
  • The derivative is the slope of the unique linear approximation that matches the function locally with error $\mathcal{O}(\Delta x^2)$.
  • Symmetric central differences achieve $\mathcal{O}(h^2)$ accuracy because odd Taylor terms cancel out.
  • Floating-point cancellation limits the usable step size; float64 achieves $\sim 10^{-8}$ error while float32 can only achieve $\sim 10^{-4}$.
  • Next up, we generalise from a single real variable $x \in \mathbb{R}$ to high-dimensional parameter spaces $\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^n$.

Chapter 2

Multivariable Calculus & Gradients

Real-world machine learning models optimize millions or billions of parameters simultaneously. Here we extend single-variable calculus to high-dimensional parameter spaces: partial derivatives, the gradient vector, directional derivatives, and the geometry of level sets.

🔗
Where you'll use this in CS: Loss surface optimization in deep neural networks, camera normal vectors in ray-tracing graphic shaders, and natural policy gradients in reinforcement learning (PPO / TRPO).
🎯 Learning Objectives
  • Assemble the gradient vector $\nabla f(\mathbf{x})$ from coordinate-wise partial derivatives.
  • Calculate directional derivatives $D_{\mathbf{u}}f(\mathbf{x}) = \nabla f(\mathbf{x}) \cdot \mathbf{u}$ for any arbitrary unit direction.
  • Prove using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality that the gradient vector points in the direction of steepest ascent and is orthogonal to level curves.
💡 3. Intuition First

Imagine standing blindfolded on a foggy mountain slope in the Western Ghats. You want to reach the peak as quickly as possible. If you take a step North, the ground rises steeply; if you take a step East, it slopes gently downward.

The partial derivatives are your step slopes in the cardinal directions (along the coordinate axes). The gradient vector combines all these directional slopes into a single compass arrow pointing straight up the mountain's steepest line of ascent. If you drop a marble, it rolls in the exact opposite direction: $-\nabla f(\mathbf{x})$.

📐 4. Formal Theory

Definition (Partial Derivative): For $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$, the partial derivative with respect to $x_i$ holds all other variables constant:

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(\mathbf{x}) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(\mathbf{x} + h \mathbf{e}_i) - f(\mathbf{x})}{h}$$

The Gradient Vector: The collection of all $n$ first partial derivatives arranged as a column vector:

$$\nabla f(\mathbf{x}) = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1} \ \vdots \ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n} \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^n$$

Directional Derivative: For any unit vector $\mathbf{u} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ ($\|\mathbf{u}\| = 1$):

$$D_{\mathbf{u}}f(\mathbf{x}) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(\mathbf{x} + h\mathbf{u}) - f(\mathbf{x})}{h} = \nabla f(\mathbf{x}) \cdot \mathbf{u} = \|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\| \cos\theta$$

Fundamental Theorems of the Gradient:

  • Steepest Ascent: Since $\cos\theta \le 1$, $D_{\mathbf{u}}f(\mathbf{x})$ is maximized when $\theta = 0 \implies \mathbf{u} = \frac{\nabla f}{\|\nabla f\|}$, with maximum rate $\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|$.
  • Steepest Descent: Minimized when $\theta = \pi \implies \mathbf{u} = -\frac{\nabla f}{\|\nabla f\|}$, with rate $-\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|$.
  • Orthogonality to Level Sets: For level set $S = \{\mathbf{x} : f(\mathbf{x}) = c\}$, $\nabla f(\mathbf{x})$ is orthogonal to every tangent vector $\mathbf{r}'(t)$ on $S$.
✍️ 5. Worked Examples (Increasing Difficulty)
Example 2.1 (Gradient of an Elliptic Paraboloid):
Let $f(x, y) = 3x^2 + 2y^2 - 4x + 6y$. Compute $\nabla f(1, -1)$ and evaluate the rate of steepest ascent.
1. $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 6x - 4 \implies \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(1, -1) = 6(1) - 4 = 2$.
2. $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 4y + 6 \implies \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(1, -1) = 4(-1) + 6 = 2$.
3. Gradient vector: $\nabla f(1, -1) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \ 2 \end{bmatrix}$.
4. Maximum rate of increase: $\|\nabla f(1, -1)\| = \sqrt{2^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt{8} \approx 2.8284$.
5. Direction of steepest ascent: $\mathbf{u}^* = \frac{1}{\sqrt{8}} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} \ 1/\sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix}$ ($45^\circ$ heading).
Example 2.2 (Directional Derivative along an Arbitrary Vector):
For $f(x, y) = x^2 y + \sin(xy)$, find the directional derivative at $(1, 0)$ in the direction of vector $\mathbf{v} = (3, 4)$.
1. First partials: $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2xy + y\cos(xy)$, $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = x^2 + x\cos(xy)$.
2. Evaluate at $(1, 0)$: $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(1, 0) = 0 + 0 = 0$; $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(1, 0) = 1^2 + 1\cos(0) = 1 + 1 = 2$.
3. Gradient vector: $\nabla f(1, 0) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \ 2 \end{bmatrix}$.
4. Normalize direction: $\|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5 \implies \mathbf{u} = \begin{bmatrix} 3/5 \ 4/5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.6 \ 0.8 \end{bmatrix}$.
5. Directional derivative: $D_{\mathbf{u}}f(1, 0) = \nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u} = 0(0.6) + 2(0.8) = 1.6$.
Example 2.3 (Binary Cross-Entropy Loss Gradient):
For prediction $\hat{y} = \sigma(\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{x})$ and target $y \in \{0, 1\}$, loss is $L(\mathbf{w}) = -[y \ln \hat{y} + (1 - y) \ln(1 - \hat{y})]$. Derive $\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} L$.
1. Let $z = \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{x} = \sum_j w_j x_j$. Then $\frac{\partial z}{\partial w_j} = x_j$.
2. Since $\hat{y} = \sigma(z)$, we have $\frac{\partial \hat{y}}{\partial z} = \hat{y}(1 - \hat{y})$.
3. $\frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat{y}} = -\frac{y}{\hat{y}} + \frac{1 - y}{1 - \hat{y}} = \frac{-y(1 - \hat{y}) + (1 - y)\hat{y}}{\hat{y}(1 - \hat{y})} = \frac{\hat{y} - y}{\hat{y}(1 - \hat{y})}$.
4. By chain rule: $\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_j} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat{y}} \cdot \frac{\partial \hat{y}}{\partial z} \cdot \frac{\partial z}{\partial w_j} = \left[\frac{\hat{y} - y}{\hat{y}(1 - \hat{y})}\right] \cdot [\hat{y}(1 - \hat{y})] \cdot x_j = (\hat{y} - y) x_j$.
5. In vector form: $\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} L = (\hat{y} - y) \mathbf{x}$. The error scalar directly scales the input vector!

⚠️ Common Student Mistakes
1. Forgetting to normalize vector $\mathbf{v}$ before computing $D_{\mathbf{u}}f$
Why it fails: The directional derivative requires a unit vector $\|\mathbf{u}\| = 1$. Using an unnormalized vector scales the derivative by the arbitrary length $\|\mathbf{v}\|$.
2. Confusing gradient direction with contour curve direction
Why it fails: The gradient vector points perpendicular ($\theta = 90^\circ$) to the contour line. Moving along the contour line yields zero change ($D_{\mathbf{u}}f = 0$).
3. Treating dependent variables as constants
Why it fails: In composite models where $y = g(x)$, you must apply the total derivative via the chain rule rather than a partial derivative that assumes $y$ is constant.
📌 Key Takeaways
  • The gradient $\nabla f(\mathbf{x})$ packs all partial derivatives into a vector pointing in the direction of steepest rate of increase.
  • The directional derivative is the dot product $D_{\mathbf{u}}f = \nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u} = \|\nabla f\| \cos\theta$.
  • Level curves are orthogonal to the gradient vector everywhere along smooth surfaces.

Chapter 3

The Chain Rule & Computational Graphs

Modern deep learning frameworks do not differentiate monolithic formulas; they represent programs as Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs) and evaluate derivatives by multiplying local Jacobian matrices through reverse-mode automatic differentiation.

🔗
Where you'll use this in CS: The autograd engines of PyTorch, TensorFlow, and JAX; robotic forward and inverse kinematics; and automatic differentiation in quantitative finance.
🎯 Learning Objectives
  • Construct computational DAGs for complex composite functions with intermediate nodes.
  • Compute Jacobian matrices $\mathbf{J} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ for vector-valued multivariable transformations.
  • Formulate reverse-mode automatic differentiation as vector-Jacobian products (VJPs) and explain why reverse mode is $\mathcal{O}(1)$ for scalar loss functions.
💡 3. Intuition First

Think of a mechanical gear train in a watch. If turning Gear A by 1 radian causes Gear B to turn 3 radians ($\frac{dB}{dA} = 3$), and turning Gear B causes Gear C to turn 2 radians ($\frac{dC}{dB} = 2$), then turning Gear A causes Gear C to turn $3 \times 2 = 6$ radians.

In high dimensions, each "gear" is a vector of numbers, and the gear ratio becomes a Jacobian matrix of all pairwise partial derivatives. The chain rule states that to find how the final output changes with respect to the input, you simply multiply all the intermediate Jacobian matrices together!

📐 4. Formal Theory

Definition (The Jacobian Matrix): For vector-valued function $\mathbf{f}: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$, the Jacobian $\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{f}} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ contains all first-order partial derivatives:

$$\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{x}) = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_n} \ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \ \frac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_n} \end{bmatrix}$$

Theorem (The Multivariable Chain Rule): Let $\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x}): \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^k$ and $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{u}): \mathbb{R}^k \to \mathbb{R}^m$. The Jacobian of the composition $(\mathbf{f} \circ \mathbf{g})$ is the matrix product:

$$\mathbf{J}_{(\mathbf{f} \circ \mathbf{g})}(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})) \cdot \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x})$$

Forward-Mode vs Reverse-Mode Automatic Differentiation:

  • Forward-Mode (JVP): Multiplies from right to left: $\mathbf{J}_k (\mathbf{J}_{k-1} \dots (\mathbf{J}_1 \mathbf{v}))$. Computes 1 column of the Jacobian per pass. Requires $\mathcal{O}(n)$ passes for $n$ inputs.
  • Reverse-Mode (VJP): Multiplies from left to right: $(((\mathbf{v}^T \mathbf{J}_k) \mathbf{J}_{k-1}) \dots \mathbf{J}_1)$. Computes 1 row of the Jacobian per pass. For a scalar loss ($m = 1$), it computes gradients for **all $N$ parameters in a single pass ($\mathcal{O}(1)$ passes)**!
✍️ 5. Worked Examples (Increasing Difficulty)
Example 3.1 (Scalar Computational Graph with Path Accumulation):
Let $z = (x + y) \cdot (y - 2)$. Decompose into a DAG and evaluate the forward and backward values at $x = 3, y = 4$.
1. Forward decomposition: $u = x + y = 3 + 4 = 7$; $v = y - 2 = 4 - 2 = 2$; $z = u \cdot v = 7 \cdot 2 = 14$.
2. Seed backward pass: $\frac{\partial z}{\partial z} = 1.0$.
3. Backward to intermediates: $\frac{\partial z}{\partial u} = \frac{\partial z}{\partial z} \cdot v = 1(2) = 2$; $\frac{\partial z}{\partial v} = \frac{\partial z}{\partial z} \cdot u = 1(7) = 7$.
4. Backward to $x$: $\frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial z}{\partial u} \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = 2(1) = 2$.
5. Backward to $y$ (accumulating across both paths!): $\frac{\partial z}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial z}{\partial u}\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial z}{\partial v}\frac{\partial v}{\partial y} = 2(1) + 7(1) = 9$.
Example 3.2 (Jacobian of Polar to Cartesian Coordinate Mapping):
Let $\mathbf{f}(r, \theta) = \begin{bmatrix} r \cos\theta \ r \sin\theta \end{bmatrix}$. Compute the Jacobian $\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{f}}$ and verify its determinant.
1. $f_1 = r \cos\theta \implies \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial r} = \cos\theta, \quad \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial \theta} = -r \sin\theta$.
2. $f_2 = r \sin\theta \implies \frac{\partial f_2}{\partial r} = \sin\theta, \quad \frac{\partial f_2}{\partial \theta} = r \cos\theta$.
3. Jacobian matrix: $\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{f}} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -r\sin\theta \ \sin\theta & r\cos\theta \end{bmatrix}$.
4. Determinant: $\det(\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{f}}) = (\cos\theta)(r\cos\theta) - (-r\sin\theta)(\sin\theta) = r\cos^2\theta + r\sin^2\theta = r(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta) = r$.
5. This proves that the infinitesimal area element in polar coordinates is $dx\,dy = |\det \mathbf{J}|\,dr\,d\theta = r\,dr\,d\theta$!
Example 3.3 (Softmax + Cross-Entropy Jacobian Multiplication):
For logits $\mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{R}^K$, softmax probabilities $\mathbf{p} = \text{softmax}(\mathbf{z})$, and cross-entropy loss $L = -\sum_k y_k \ln p_k$, prove by the multivariable chain rule that $\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{z}} = \mathbf{p} - \mathbf{y}$.
1. Upstream loss gradient vector w.r.t probabilities: $\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{p}} = \left[-\frac{y_1}{p_1}, \dots, -\frac{y_K}{p_K}\right]$.
2. The Softmax Jacobian is $\mathbf{J}_{\text{softmax}} = \text{diag}(\mathbf{p}) - \mathbf{p}\mathbf{p}^T$. Component: $\frac{\partial p_j}{\partial z_i} = p_i(\delta_{ij} - p_j)$.
3. VJP product: $\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_i} = \sum_j \frac{\partial L}{\partial p_j} \frac{\partial p_j}{\partial z_i} = \sum_j \left(-\frac{y_j}{p_j}\right) p_i(\delta_{ij} - p_j)$.
4. Expand: $= -\frac{y_i}{p_i} p_i(1 - p_i) - \sum_{j \ne i} \frac{y_j}{p_j} (-p_j p_i) = -y_i(1 - p_i) + p_i \sum_{j \ne i} y_j$.
5. Since targets sum to $1$ ($\sum_{j \ne i} y_j = 1 - y_i$): $= -y_i + y_i p_i + p_i - p_i y_i = p_i - y_i$ (Q.E.D.).

⚠️ Common Student Mistakes
1. Forgetting to accumulate gradients at branch nodes
Why it fails: If variable $y$ flows into both $u$ and $v$, setting $\frac{\partial z}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial z}{\partial u}\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}$ misses the entire second path through $v$. Always sum incoming gradients ($+=$).
2. Multiplying Jacobians in the wrong order
Why it fails: Matrix multiplication is non-commutative ($\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B} \ne \mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}$). Reversing order results in dimensional mismatch or invalid derivatives.
3. Confusing Automatic Differentiation with Symbolic Differentiation
Why it fails: Symbolic differentiation generates massive algebraic expressions that explode in memory (expression swell). AD evaluates numerical values directly at each node.
📌 Key Takeaways
  • Computational graphs break down complex non-linear programs into primitive operations with known local derivatives.
  • Reverse-mode automatic differentiation computes all parameter gradients in a single backward pass ($\mathcal{O}(1)$ passes).
  • When an input splits into multiple branches, its total derivative is the exact sum of contributions across all directed paths.

Backpropagation by Hand

No student truly understands deep learning until they have computed an exact numerical forward and backward pass by hand with pen and paper, verified every decimal place, and confirmed the analytical gradients against a finite-difference gradient check.

🔗

💡 3. Intuition First

Think of backpropagation as credit and blame assignment. Imagine an ensemble software team delivers a software release with an unexpected bug. To fix the bug, the engineering manager doesn't just blame the person who pushed the final commit; they trace back through the git history and architecture diagrams.

In a neural network, the output error $\hat{y} - y$ is the "bug". Backpropagation uses the chain rule to work backward from the output layer, asking: "How much did hidden neuron 1 contribute to this mistake? How much did weight $w_{11}$ contribute?" Every parameter receives an update proportional to its direct responsibility.

📐 4. Formal Theory

Network Architecture:

  • Input: $\mathbf{x} = [x_1, x_2]^T \in \mathbb{R}^2$. Target: $y \in \mathbb{R}$.
  • Hidden Layer: $\mathbf{z}_1 = \mathbf{W}_1 \mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}_1 \in \mathbb{R}^2$, with activation $\mathbf{a}_1 = \sigma(\mathbf{z}_1)$.
  • Output Layer: $z_2 = \mathbf{W}_2 \mathbf{a}_1 + b_2 \in \mathbb{R}$, with prediction $\hat{y} = \sigma(z_2)$.
  • Loss Function: $L = \frac{1}{2}(\hat{y} - y)^2$.

The Exact Backpropagation Equations:

$$\delta_2 = \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_2} = (\hat{y} - y) \cdot \sigma'(z_2) = (\hat{y} - y)\hat{y}(1 - \hat{y})$$ $$\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{W}_2} = \delta_2 \mathbf{a}_1^T, \quad \frac{\partial L}{\partial b_2} = \delta_2$$ $$\boldsymbol{\delta}_1 = (\mathbf{W}_2^T \delta_2) \odot \sigma'(\mathbf{z}_1) = (\mathbf{W}_2^T \delta_2) \odot \mathbf{a}_1 \odot (1 - \mathbf{a}_1)$$ $$\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{W}_1} = \boldsymbol{\delta}_1 \mathbf{x}^T, \quad \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{b}_1} = \boldsymbol{\delta}_1$$

Gradient Checking Formula (Relative Error):

$$\text{RelErr} = \frac{\|\nabla_{\text{num}} - \nabla_{\text{analytic}}\|_2}{\|\nabla_{\text{num}}\|_2 + \|\nabla_{\text{analytic}}\|_2 + 10^{-8}} < 10^{-7}$$

⚠️ Common Student Mistakes
1. Forgetting the activation derivative term $\sigma'(z)$
Why it fails: Setting $\delta = \hat{y} - y$ is only valid when using identity output or Softmax with cross-entropy. For mean squared error with sigmoid, you MUST include $\hat{y}(1 - \hat{y})$.

Chapter 5

Convexity & The Hessian Matrix

Convexity is the dividing line between tractable and intractable optimization problems. In this chapter, we develop the geometric intuition of convex sets and functions, supporting hyperplanes, and the second-order Hessian test.

🔗
Where you'll use this in CS: Support Vector Machine (SVM) Quadratic Programming solvers, linear programming in cloud resource allocation (Simplex), and logistic regression guarantees.
🎯 Learning Objectives
  • Prove whether a given set $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ or function $f(\mathbf{x})$ is convex using line-segment definitions.
  • Apply the first-order supporting hyperplane condition and the second-order Hessian eigenvalue test $\nabla^2 f(\mathbf{x}) \succeq 0$.
  • Prove the Fundamental Theorem of Convex Optimisation: every local minimum is a global minimum.
💡 3. Intuition First

Think of the difference between a smooth salad bowl and an egg carton. If you place a marble anywhere inside a salad bowl, no matter where you let go, it rolls down to the exact same unique resting point at the bottom.

In an egg carton, there are dozens of different dips and cups. If you release the marble, it gets trapped in whichever local dip it was dropped into. Convex functions are "salad bowls" in $N$ dimensions: they never bend down, never create deceptive false bottoms, and guarantee that any local descent path reaches the one true global minimum.

📐 4. Formal Theory

Definition (Convex Set): A set $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is convex if for all $\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \in S$ and all $\theta \in [0, 1]$:

$$\theta \mathbf{x} + (1 - \theta) \mathbf{y} \in S$$

Definition (Convex Function): A function $f: S \to \mathbb{R}$ defined on a convex set $S$ is convex if for all $\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \in S$ and $\theta \in [0, 1]$:

$$f(\theta \mathbf{x} + (1 - \theta)\mathbf{y}) \le \theta f(\mathbf{x}) + (1 - \theta) f(\mathbf{y})$$

First-Order Condition (Supporting Hyperplanes): $f$ is convex if and only if for all $\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \in S$:

$$f(\mathbf{y}) \ge f(\mathbf{x}) + \nabla f(\mathbf{x})^T (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{x})$$

Second-Order Condition (The Hessian Matrix): The Hessian matrix $\mathbf{H} = \nabla^2 f(\mathbf{x}) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ where $H_{ij} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i \partial x_j}$. $f$ is convex if and only if the Hessian is positive semi-definite everywhere:

$$\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x}) \succeq 0 \iff \forall \mathbf{v} \ne \mathbf{0}, \; \mathbf{v}^T \mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x}) \mathbf{v} \ge 0 \iff \text{All eigenvalues } \lambda_i \ge 0$$

Fundamental Theorem of Convex Optimisation: Let $f: S \to \mathbb{R}$ be a convex function on a convex set $S$. If $\mathbf{x}^* \in S$ is a local minimizer of $f$, then $\mathbf{x}^*$ is a global minimizer of $f$. Furthermore, if $f$ is strictly convex ($\mathbf{H} \succ 0$), the global minimizer is unique!

✍️ 5. Worked Examples (Increasing Difficulty)
Example 5.1 (Convexity of a Euclidean Ball):
Prove that the closed unit ball $B = \{\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n : \|\mathbf{x}\|_2 \le 1\}$ is a convex set.
1. Let $\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \in B$ and scalar $\theta \in [0, 1]$.
2. By definition of $B$, $\|\mathbf{x}\|_2 \le 1$ and $\|\mathbf{y}\|_2 \le 1$.
3. Consider the combination $\mathbf{z} = \theta \mathbf{x} + (1 - \theta)\mathbf{y}$.
4. By the triangle inequality and absolute homogeneity of norms:
  $\|\mathbf{z}\|_2 = \|\theta \mathbf{x} + (1 - \theta)\mathbf{y}\|_2 \le \|\theta \mathbf{x}\|_2 + \|(1 - \theta)\mathbf{y}\|_2 = \theta \|\mathbf{x}\|_2 + (1 - \theta)\|\mathbf{y}\|_2$.
5. Since $\|\mathbf{x}\|_2 \le 1$ and $\|\mathbf{y}\|_2 \le 1$: $\|\mathbf{z}\|_2 \le \theta(1) + (1 - \theta)(1) = 1$.
6. Therefore $\mathbf{z} \in B$, proving $B$ is a convex set (Q.E.D.).
Example 5.2 (Hessian Eigenvalue Test for Multivariable Convexity):
Check whether $f(x, y) = 2x^2 - 4xy + 5y^2 + 6x - 2y$ is strictly convex by computing its Hessian matrix $\mathbf{H}$.
1. First partials: $f_x = 4x - 4y + 6, \quad f_y = -4x + 10y - 2$.
2. Second partials: $f_{xx} = 4, \quad f_{yy} = 10, \quad f_{xy} = f_{yx} = -4$.
3. Hessian matrix: $\mathbf{H} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & -4 \ -4 & 10 \end{bmatrix}$.
4. Eigenvalues solve $\det(\mathbf{H} - \lambda \mathbf{I}) = (4 - \lambda)(10 - \lambda) - 16 = \lambda^2 - 14\lambda + 24 = (\lambda - 2)(\lambda - 12) = 0$.
5. Eigenvalues are $\lambda_1 = 2 > 0$ and $\lambda_2 = 12 > 0$.
6. Since all eigenvalues are strictly positive, $\mathbf{H} \succ 0$ everywhere, proving $f$ is strictly convex.
Example 5.3 (Convexity of Negative Entropy on Probability Simplex):
Prove that the negative entropy function $f(\mathbf{p}) = \sum_{i=1}^n p_i \ln p_i$ is strictly convex on the open probability simplex $\Delta = \{\mathbf{p} \in \mathbb{R}^n : \sum p_i = 1, p_i > 0\}$.
1. First partial derivatives: $\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i} = \ln p_i + p_i \cdot \frac{1}{p_i} = \ln p_i + 1$.
2. Second partial derivatives: $\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial p_i^2} = \frac{1}{p_i}$, and for $i \ne j$: $\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial p_i \partial p_j} = 0$.
3. The Hessian is purely diagonal: $\mathbf{H} = \text{diag}\left(\frac{1}{p_1}, \frac{1}{p_2}, \dots, \frac{1}{p_n}\right)$.
4. For any non-zero vector $\mathbf{v} \ne \mathbf{0}$: $\mathbf{v}^T \mathbf{H} \mathbf{v} = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{v_i^2}{p_i}$.
5. Since each $p_i > 0$ and $v_i^2 \ge 0$ (with at least one $v_i^2 > 0$), $\mathbf{v}^T \mathbf{H} \mathbf{v} > 0$.
6. Thus $\mathbf{H} \succ 0$, proving negative entropy is strictly convex (and Shannon entropy $-f(\mathbf{p})$ is strictly concave!).

⚠️ Common Student Mistakes
1. Checking only diagonal entries $f_{xx} > 0$ and $f_{yy} > 0$
Why it fails: Positive diagonal entries alone do NOT ensure convexity. A saddle point like $f(x, y) = x^2 - 4xy + y^2$ has $f_{xx} = 2 > 0$ and $f_{yy} = 2 > 0$, but is NOT convex because the off-diagonal cross-terms dominate (eigenvalues $-2$ and $6$). You MUST check all eigenvalues of $\mathbf{H}$.
2. Assuming deep neural network loss surfaces are convex
Why it fails: Due to permutation symmetries of hidden neurons and non-linear activations, neural net loss surfaces are profoundly non-convex, containing vast numbers of saddle points.
3. Confusing convex sets with convex functions
Why it fails: A set is a collection of points (e.g. a sphere or polygon); a function is a rule that assigns a real number to each point. A function can only be convex if its domain is a convex set.
📌 Key Takeaways

  • First-order condition: tangent hyperplanes globally underestimate the function everywhere.
  • Second-order condition: the Hessian matrix $\nabla^2 f(\mathbf{x})$ must be positive semi-definite ($\lambda_i \ge 0$).

Chapter 6

Gradient-Based Optimisation & Adam

How do modern deep learning models actually train in practice? We contrast Full Batch Gradient Descent with Stochastic Gradient Descent, dissect Polyak Momentum and RMSprop, and derive the Adam and AdamW optimizers.

🔗
Where you'll use this in CS: Pre-training Large Language Models (LLMs) with AdamW in PyTorch/DeepSpeed, fine-tuning Stable Diffusion models, and tuning learning-rate warmup schedules.
🎯 Learning Objectives
  • Contrast Batch GD, Mini-batch SGD, and Pure SGD in computational complexity $\mathcal{O}(N)$ vs gradient variance.
  • Analyze how Momentum dampens high-frequency oscillations in ill-conditioned ravines ($\kappa \gg 1$).
  • Implement the Adam optimizer update formulas: first moments, second moments, bias corrections, and decoupled weight decay (AdamW).
💡 3. Intuition First

Imagine rolling down an icy canyon whose side walls are 100 times steeper than the gentle downward slope of the canyon floor. A standard lightweight ball (Vanilla GD) bounces violently back and forth between the canyon walls, making almost zero forward progress.

Now imagine giving that ball heavy physical momentum: its lateral bounces cancel each other out, while its forward velocity accelerates down the canyon floor! Next, equip the ball with adaptive traction spikes (RMSprop / Adam) that individually dampen sensitivity on the steep axis while amplifying sensitivity on the flat axis. That is the genius of modern adaptive optimizers.

📐 4. Formal Theory

1. Vanilla Gradient Descent: Update rule with step size $\eta > 0$:

$$\mathbf{x}_{t+1} = \mathbf{x}_t - \eta \nabla f(\mathbf{x}_t)$$

Convergence condition for $L$-smooth function ($\|\nabla^2 f\|_2 \le L$): The learning rate must strictly satisfy $0 < \eta < \frac{2}{L}$. If $\eta > \frac{2}{L}$, iterates diverge to infinity!

2. Polyak Momentum: Tracks velocity vector $\mathbf{v}_t$ with decay $\beta \in [0.8, 0.99]$:

$$\mathbf{v}_{t+1} = \beta \mathbf{v}_t + \nabla f(\mathbf{x}_t), \quad \mathbf{x}_{t+1} = \mathbf{x}_t - \eta \mathbf{v}_{t+1}$$

3. The Adam Optimizer (Adaptive Moment Estimation):

$$\mathbf{m}_t = \beta_1 \mathbf{m}_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_1)\mathbf{g}_t \quad (\text{1st Moment: Mean})$$ $$\mathbf{v}_t = \beta_2 \mathbf{v}_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_2)\mathbf{g}_t^{\odot 2} \quad (\text{2nd Moment: Uncentered Variance})$$ $$\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{m}_t}{1 - \beta_1^t}, \quad \hat{\mathbf{v}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{v}_t}{1 - \beta_2^t} \quad (\text{Bias Corrections})$$ $$\mathbf{x}_{t+1} = \mathbf{x}_t - \frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t} + \epsilon} \odot \hat{\mathbf{m}}_t$$

Why bias correction is required: Because $\mathbf{m}_0 = \mathbf{0}$ and $\mathbf{v}_0 = \mathbf{0}$, uncorrected moments are heavily shrunk toward zero in early iterations (e.g. at $t=1$, $\mathbf{m}_1 = (1-\beta_1)\mathbf{g}_1 = 0.1 \mathbf{g}_1$). Dividing by $1 - \beta_1^t = 0.1$ restores the true unbiased expectation $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t] = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{g}_t]$.

✍️ 5. Worked Examples (Increasing Difficulty)
Example 6.1 (Learning Rate Divergence on 1D Quadratic):
Let $f(x) = 5x^2$ ($L = 10$). Trace 3 steps of Gradient Descent from $x_0 = 1$ with (a) $\eta = 0.10$ and (b) $\eta = 0.25$.

Let $f(x, y) = 10x^2 + y^2$. Compute Step 1 and Step 2 of Gradient Descent vs Momentum ($\beta = 0.9$) with $\eta = 0.05$ starting from $(1, 1)$.
1. Initial gradient at $(1, 1)$: $\mathbf{g}_0 = [20, 2]^T$.
2. Vanilla GD Step 1: $\mathbf{x}_1 = [1, 1]^T - 0.05[20, 2]^T = [1 - 1.0, 1 - 0.10]^T = [0.0, 0.90]^T$.
3. Gradient at $\mathbf{x}_1$: $\mathbf{g}_1 = [20(0), 2(0.90)]^T = [0, 1.80]^T$.

6. Momentum Step 2: $\mathbf{v}_2 = 0.9[20, 2]^T + [0, 1.80]^T = [18.0, 3.60]^T \implies \mathbf{x}_2 = [0, 0.90]^T - 0.05[18.0, 3.60]^T = [-0.90, 0.72]^T$.
7. The momentum along the gentle $y$-axis accelerated from $0.10$ to $0.18$ advance!
Example 6.3 (Complete Numerical Trace of Step 1 of Adam):
Given initial point $x_0 = 5.0$, loss $f(x) = x^2$, gradient $g_1 = 10.0$. Trace Step 1 of Adam with $\beta_1 = 0.9, \beta_2 = 0.999, \eta = 0.10, \epsilon = 10^{-8}$.
1. First moment: $m_1 = 0.9(0) + (1 - 0.9)(10.0) = 0.1(10.0) = 1.0$.
2. Second moment: $v_1 = 0.999(0) + (1 - 0.999)(10.0^2) = 0.001(100.0) = 0.10$.
3. Bias-corrected first moment: $\hat{m}_1 = \frac{1.0}{1 - 0.9^1} = \frac{1.0}{0.1} = 10.0$.
4. Bias-corrected second moment: $\hat{v}_1 = \frac{0.10}{1 - 0.999^1} = \frac{0.10}{0.001} = 100.0$.
5. Parameter update: $\Delta x_1 = -\eta \frac{\hat{m}_1}{\sqrt{\hat{v}_1} + \epsilon} = -0.10 \frac{10.0}{\sqrt{100.0}} = -0.10 \frac{10.0}{10.0} = -0.10$.
6. New position: $x_1 = 5.0 - 0.10 = 4.90$. Notice how Adam automatically normalized the raw gradient ($10.0$) so the initial step size exactly matches $\eta = 0.10$!

# Output: Step 1: x = [3.900, 3.900] | Step 2: x = [3.800, 3.800] | Step 3: x = [3.700, 3.700]
⚠️ Common Student Mistakes
1. Omitting bias corrections ($1 - \beta^t$) in Adam
Why it fails: Because moments are initialized to zero, early updates without bias correction are orders of magnitude too small, freezing training in early epochs.
2. Applying L2 regularization as weight decay inside Adam
Why it fails: Adding L2 penalty directly to the loss causes weight decay to be divided by the adaptive second-moment denominator $\sqrt{\mathbf{v}_t}$. This is why LLM training universally uses **AdamW**, which applies weight decay directly to parameter updates.
3. Setting learning rate without checking Lipschitz constant $L$
Why it fails: If $\eta > 2/L$, gradient descent provably diverges. Always monitor loss curves for explosive `NaN` values indicating learning rate overshoot.
📌 Key Takeaways
  • Vanilla Gradient Descent struggles in ravines where the Hessian condition number $\kappa \gg 1$.
  • Momentum accumulates velocity across consistent directions, achieving an acceleration factor of $\frac{1}{1 - \beta}$.
  • Adam combines Momentum and RMSprop with bias corrections, adapting individual step sizes for each coordinate.

Chapter 7

Constrained Optimisation & KKT Conditions

Real engineering problems rarely allow parameters to take arbitrary values; models must respect resource budgets, probabilities must sum to one, and classifiers must enforce geometric margins. We master Lagrange multipliers, KKT conditions, and the Support Vector Machine dual formulation.

🔗
Where you'll use this in CS: Support Vector Machine (SVM) max-margin hyperplanes, Wasserstein GAN gradient penalties (1-Lipschitz constraints), and distributed cloud resource optimization.
🎯 Learning Objectives
  • Formulate equality-constrained optimization problems using the Lagrangian function $\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\lambda})$.
  • State and interpret all 4 Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions for inequality-constrained optimization.
  • Derive the dual formulation of Support Vector Machines and explain how complementary slackness reveals the sparse set of support vectors.
💡 3. Intuition First

Imagine walking across a grassy park trying to go as far North as possible, but you are physically constrained to walk along a curved walking path. Where do you stop?

You stop at the exact point where the walking path curves and points purely East-West! At that point, any further step along the path would start taking you South. Geometrically, the direction of desired improvement ($\nabla f$) points directly perpendicular to the path ($\nabla g$). The gradients become perfectly collinear: $\nabla f = -\lambda \nabla g$.

📐 4. Formal Theory

1. Equality Constraints (Lagrange Multipliers):

Minimize $f(\mathbf{x})$ subject to $g_i(\mathbf{x}) = 0$ for $i = 1, \dots, m$. The Lagrangian is:

$$\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\lambda}) = f(\mathbf{x}) + \sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i g_i(\mathbf{x})$$

Stationarity requires setting all partial derivatives to zero: $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \mathcal{L} = \mathbf{0}$ and $\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}} \mathcal{L} = \mathbf{0}$.

2. Inequality Constraints & The KKT Conditions:

Minimize $f(\mathbf{x})$ subject to $g_i(\mathbf{x}) = 0$ ($i = 1 \dots m$) and $h_j(\mathbf{x}) \le 0$ ($j = 1 \dots k$). For local minimum $\mathbf{x}^*$, there exist multipliers $\boldsymbol{\lambda}, \boldsymbol{\mu}$ satisfying:

$$\text{1. Stationarity:} \quad \nabla f(\mathbf{x}^*) + \sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i \nabla g_i(\mathbf{x}^*) + \sum_{j=1}^k \mu_j \nabla h_j(\mathbf{x}^*) = \mathbf{0}$$ $$\text{2. Primal Feasibility:} \quad g_i(\mathbf{x}^*) = 0, \quad h_j(\mathbf{x}^*) \le 0$$ $$\text{3. Dual Feasibility:} \quad \mu_j \ge 0 \quad (\forall j)$$ $$\text{4. Complementary Slackness:} \quad \mu_j \cdot h_j(\mathbf{x}^*) = 0 \quad (\forall j)$$

Complementary Slackness Intuition: Either the constraint is active ($h_j(\mathbf{x}^*) = 0$), in which case its multiplier $\mu_j > 0$ acts as a holding force; or the constraint is inactive ($h_j(\mathbf{x}^*) < 0$), in which case the boundary does not restrict the minimum and its multiplier is strictly zero ($\mu_j = 0$).

✍️ 5. Worked Examples (Increasing Difficulty)
Example 7.1 (Lagrange Multipliers with Equality Constraint):
Minimize $f(x, y) = x^2 + 2y^2$ subject to the linear equality constraint $x + y = 3$.
1. Lagrangian: $\mathcal{L}(x, y, \lambda) = x^2 + 2y^2 + \lambda(x + y - 3)$.
2. Stationarity equations:
  $\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial x} = 2x + \lambda = 0 \implies x = -\frac{\lambda}{2}$.
  $\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial y} = 4y + \lambda = 0 \implies y = -\frac{\lambda}{4}$.
3. Substitute into constraint: $\left(-\frac{\lambda}{2}\right) + \left(-\frac{\lambda}{4}\right) = 3 \implies -\frac{3\lambda}{4} = 3 \implies \lambda = -4$.
4. Solve coordinates: $x^* = -\frac{-4}{2} = 2$, $y^* = -\frac{-4}{4} = 1$.
5. Minimum value: $f(2, 1) = 2^2 + 2(1^2) = 4 + 2 = 6$.
Example 7.2 (Inequality Constraint with KKT Case Analysis):
Solve $\min f(x) = (x - 4)^2$ subject to inequality constraint $x \le 2$ using KKT conditions.
1. Rewrite constraint as $h(x) = x - 2 \le 0$. Lagrangian: $\mathcal{L}(x, \mu) = (x - 4)^2 + \mu(x - 2)$.
2. KKT conditions: (1) $2(x - 4) + \mu = 0$; (2) $x \le 2$; (3) $\mu \ge 0$; (4) $\mu(x - 2) = 0$.
3. Test Case A (Inactive $\mu = 0$): $2(x - 4) + 0 = 0 \implies x = 4$. But $4 \not\le 2$, violating primal feasibility!
4. Test Case B (Active $x = 2$): $2(2 - 4) + \mu = 0 \implies -4 + \mu = 0 \implies \mu = 4$.
5. Since $\mu = 4 \ge 0$, all KKT conditions are satisfied! Minimum is at $x^* = 2$ with cost $f(2) = (2-4)^2 = 4$.
Example 7.3 (Support Vector Machine Dual Derivation):
Derive the SVM dual formulation from primal margin maximization $\min_{\mathbf{w}, b} \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2$ subject to $y_i(\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{x}_i + b) \ge 1$.
1. Primal Lagrangian: $\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}, b, \boldsymbol{\alpha}) = \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2 - \sum_{i=1}^N \alpha_i [y_i(\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{x}_i + b) - 1]$ with $\alpha_i \ge 0$.
2. Stationarity w.r.t $\mathbf{w}$: $\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} \mathcal{L} = \mathbf{w} - \sum_i \alpha_i y_i \mathbf{x}_i = \mathbf{0} \implies \mathbf{w}^* = \sum_{i=1}^N \alpha_i y_i \mathbf{x}_i$.
3. Stationarity w.r.t $b$: $\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial b} = -\sum_i \alpha_i y_i = 0 \implies \sum_{i=1}^N \alpha_i y_i = 0$.
4. Substitute $\mathbf{w}^*$ back into $\mathcal{L}$: $\frac{1}{2}\left\|\sum_i \alpha_i y_i \mathbf{x}_i\right\|^2 - \sum_i \alpha_i y_i \left(\sum_j \alpha_j y_j \mathbf{x}_j\right)^T \mathbf{x}_i + \sum_i \alpha_i$.
5. Simplifies to the famous SVM Dual Problem: $\max_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}} \sum_{i=1}^N \alpha_i - \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i,j} \alpha_i \alpha_j y_i y_j (\mathbf{x}_i \cdot \mathbf{x}_j)$ subject to $\alpha_i \ge 0$ and $\sum \alpha_i y_i = 0$.
6. By complementary slackness, only data points directly on the margin boundary have $\alpha_i > 0$: these are the **Support Vectors**!

from scipy.optimize import minimize
# Minimize f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2 subject to x + 2y - 4 = 0
def obj(x): return x[0]**2 + x[1]**2
constraints = ({'type': 'eq', 'fun': lambda x: x[0] + 2*x[1] - 4})
res = minimize(obj, [0.0, 0.0], constraints=constraints)
x_opt, y_opt = res.x
print(f"Optimal (x, y): ({x_opt:.3f}, {y_opt:.3f}) | Min value: {res.fun:.3f}")

⚠️ Common Student Mistakes
1. Assuming inactive inequality constraints have non-zero multipliers
Why it fails: By KKT complementary slackness, if $h_j(\mathbf{x}) < 0$, the multiplier MUST be $\mu_j = 0$. Multipliers can only be positive if the constraint is actively binding ($h_j = 0$).
2. Allowing negative multipliers on inequality constraints ($\mu < 0$)
Why it fails: A negative multiplier would push the solution AWAY from the boundary into the forbidden region. Dual feasibility strictly enforces $\mu_j \ge 0$.
3. Forgetting constraint qualification (e.g. Slater's condition)
Why it fails: KKT conditions are only guaranteed to hold if the constraint gradients are linearly independent or if a strictly interior feasible point exists (Slater's condition).
📌 Key Takeaways
  • Equality constraints require gradients to be collinear: $\nabla f = -\lambda \nabla g$.
  • The KKT conditions generalize Lagrange multipliers to inequality constraints through primal feasibility, dual feasibility, and complementary slackness.
  • Complementary slackness produces sparse models in Support Vector Machines, where only support vectors determine the separating hyperplane.
