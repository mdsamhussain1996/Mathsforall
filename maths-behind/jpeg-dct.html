How JPEG Shrinks Photos

The Discrete Cosine Transform & Perceptual Quantization

A raw 12-megapixel smartphone photo takes roughly 36 megabytes of storage. When saved as a JPEG, the exact same photo shrinks down to just 2.5 megabytes—a staggering 93% compression ratio—yet when you look at it on your screen, your eyes can hardly spot a difference. Where did the missing 33 megabytes go?

💡 1. The Big Idea

The Core Intuition

The human eye is an imperfect optical sensor: we notice slow, smooth changes in lighting immediately, but our brains are practically blind to fine, rapid high-frequency zig-zags. JPEG cuts an image into $8 \times 8$ pixel tiles, converts them from spatial coordinates to 64 cosine frequency amounts, and then ruthlessly divides all the fine high frequencies to zero (quantization). The zeros compress away to almost nothing, while the image still looks sharp and crisp to human eyes!

📐 2. The Formal Mathematics

Each $8 \times 8$ pixel block $f(x,y)$ is transformed into 64 frequency coefficients $F(u,v)$ via the 2D Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT):

$$F(u,v) = \frac{1}{4} C(u) C(v) \sum_{x=0}^{7} \sum_{y=0}^{7} f(x,y) \cos\left[\frac{(2x+1)u\pi}{16}\right] \cos\left[\frac{(2y+1)v\pi}{16}\right]$$

The top-left coefficient $F(0,0)$ is the DC component (the overall average brightness). The remaining 63 are AC components (increasing in frequency down and right). JPEG then divides by a psychovisual quantization matrix $Q$ and rounds:

$$F_Q(u,v) = \text{round}\left( \frac{F(u,v)}{Q(u,v)} \right) \implies \text{Almost all high-frequency terms turn to } 0$$
📝 Worked Example by Hand

Let's compress a simplified $2 \times 2$ pixel tile: $\begin{bmatrix} 120 & 130 \\ 120 & 130 \end{bmatrix}$ (a gentle horizontal brightness step):

Step 1: Compute Average (DC) and Difference (AC) Basis
$$F(0,0) = \frac{120 + 130 + 120 + 130}{2} = 250 \;\text{(Average brightness)}$$ $$F(0,1) = \frac{(120 - 130) + (120 - 130)}{2} = -10 \;\text{(Horizontal gradient)}$$ $$F(1,0) = 0, \quad F(1,1) = 0 \;\text{(Vertical & diagonal changes)}$$
Step 2: Quantize with $Q = \begin{bmatrix} 10 & 20 \\ 20 & 40 \end{bmatrix}$
$$F_Q(0,0) = \text{round}(250 / 10) = 25$$ $$F_Q(0,1) = \text{round}(-10 / 20) = 0 \;\text{(Rounded to zero!)}$$
Step 3: Reconstruct via Inverse DCT
De-quantize: $25 \times 10 = 250$. Reconstructed pixels: $[125, 125, 125, 125]$.
Conclusion: The difference between $120$ and $125$ is virtually imperceptible to human rods and cones, but storage dropped from 4 independent numbers to just 1!

🐍 4. Try It in Python (< 15 Lines)

Python 3 Runnable Snippet
import numpy as np

# 8x8 image block
block = np.array([[120 + i*5 + j*5 for j in range(8)] for i in range(8)])

# 1D DCT matrix
N = 8
A = np.zeros((N, N))
for i in range(N):
    for j in range(N):
        A[i, j] = np.sqrt(1/N if i==0 else 2/N) * np.cos((2*j+1)*i*np.pi / (2*N))

# 2D DCT: F = A * block * A.T
dct_coeffs = A @ (block - 128) @ A.T
# Zero out high frequency bottom-right quadrant
dct_coeffs[4:, 4:] = 0
reconstructed = (A.T @ dct_coeffs @ A) + 128
print("Max absolute pixel reconstruction error:", np.max(np.abs(block - reconstructed)))
