How Netflix Guesses Your Next Show

Low-Rank SVD, Latent Taste Factors & Collaborative Filtering

Netflix has 250 million subscribers and over 15,000 movie titles. The average user has watched fewer than 150 movies. That means if you drew a giant table of [Users $\times$ Movies], over 99.2% of the boxes are completely empty! How does Netflix accurately predict whether you will give a 5-star rating to a movie you've never heard of?

💡 1. The Big Idea

The Core Intuition

People don't pick movies at random, and movies aren't isolated entities. Both exist in a low-dimensional space of latent factors. A movie might be $80\%$ sci-fi, $10\%$ action, $10\%$ drama. A user might have a taste vector that loves sci-fi and hates romance. If we compress the giant $250\text{M} \times 15\text{k}$ sparse rating matrix into two tiny dense matrices—User Tastes ($P$) and Movie Traits ($Q$)—taking their dot product fills in every single blank in the entire database!

📐 2. The Formal Mathematics

This is the core insight of Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) and low-rank matrix factorisation (which won the famous $1,000,000 Netflix Prize):

$$R \approx P \cdot Q^T, \qquad \text{where } P \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times k}, \; Q \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k}, \; k \ll \min(m, n)$$ $$\hat{r}_{ui} = \mathbf{p}_u \cdot \mathbf{q}_i = \sum_{f=1}^{k} p_{uf} \cdot q_{if}$$

To learn the latent factors from sparse known ratings, we minimize regularised squared error using stochastic gradient descent (SGD):

$$\mathcal{L} = \sum_{(u,i) \in \text{known}} (r_{ui} - \mathbf{p}_u \cdot \mathbf{q}_i)^2 + \lambda \left( \|\mathbf{p}_u\|^2 + \|\mathbf{q}_i\|^2 \right)$$
📝 Worked Example by Hand

Suppose we discover $k=2$ latent factors: Factor 1: Sci-Fi affinity, Factor 2: Animation affinity. Ratings are scaled $1$ to $5$ stars:

Step 1: User Tastes for Alice ($\mathbf{p}$) and Movie Traits for Interstellar ($\mathbf{q}$)
Alice loves sci-fi, avoids cartoons: $\mathbf{p}_{\text{Alice}} = [0.9, 0.1]^T$.
Interstellar is pure sci-fi: $\mathbf{q}_{\text{Interstellar}} = [0.95, 0.05]^T$.
Step 2: Compute Predicted Rating via Dot Product
$$\hat{r} = 5 \cdot (\mathbf{p} \cdot \mathbf{q}) = 5 \cdot \big[ (0.9)(0.95) + (0.1)(0.05) \big] = 5 \cdot [0.855 + 0.005] = 5 \cdot (0.86) = 4.3 \text{ stars!}$$
Step 3: What about Finding Nemo? ($\mathbf{q} = [0.05, 0.95]^T$)
$$\hat{r} = 5 \cdot \big[ (0.9)(0.05) + (0.1)(0.95) \big] = 5 \cdot [0.045 + 0.095] = 5 \cdot (0.14) = 0.7 \text{ stars (Hide from homepage)}$$ Conclusion: Two numbers per movie and user accurately predict personalized satisfaction for millions of catalog entries!

🐍 4. Try It in Python (< 15 Lines)

Python 3 Runnable Snippet
import numpy as np

# Sparse ratings: -1 means unrated
R = np.array([
    [5, -1, 1, -1],
    [4,  5, -1, 1],
    [-1, 1,  5, 5],
    [1, -1,  4, 5]
])

# 2 latent factors: Sci-Fi, Animation
P = np.array([[0.9, 0.1], [0.85, 0.2], [0.1, 0.95], [0.2, 0.8]])
Q = np.array([[0.95, 0.05], [0.9, 0.1], [0.1, 0.9], [0.05, 0.95]])

# Reconstructed rating matrix
R_pred = np.clip(np.round((P @ Q.T) * 5, 1), 1.0, 5.0)
print("Predicted Missing Ratings:\n", R_pred)
