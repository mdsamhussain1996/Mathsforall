How Shazam Recognises a Song

The Fast Fourier Transform, Spectrograms & Combinatorial Hashes

You are standing inside a noisy restaurant with blaring chatter and clinking wine glasses. A 4-second fragment of a song plays faintly in the background. You tap Shazam on your phone, and within 2 seconds it correctly identifies the track, album, and exact second playing. How does it hear through all that noise?

💡 1. The Big Idea

The Core Intuition

A raw sound recording is just a wiggly line of pressure measurements over time (44,100 samples per second). Trying to match that raw wave is hopeless: background noise, echoes, and volume corrupt the time wave completely. But in 1822, Joseph Fourier discovered that any periodic wave can be decomposed into a unique sum of pure sine and cosine tones. When you convert audio into frequencies over time (a spectrogram), a song's musical chords form a persistent constellation of frequency peaks that survive any amount of chatter!

📐 2. The Formal Mathematics

The Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) translates $N$ time samples $x[n]$ into $N$ frequency coefficients $X[k]$:

$$X[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n] \cdot e^{-i 2\pi k n / N} = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n] \Big[ \cos\Big(\frac{2\pi kn}{N}\Big) - i \sin\Big(\frac{2\pi kn}{N}\Big) \Big]$$

By sliding a window over time (the Short-Time Fourier Transform, or STFT), Shazam generates a 2D time-frequency spectrogram. It picks out the highest energy peaks (constellation points) and pairs them into compact combinatorial hash signatures:

$$\text{Hash} = \big[ f_1 \;:\; f_2 \;:\; (t_2 - t_1) \big] \implies \text{Points to Song ID and Time offset } t_1$$
📝 Worked Example by Hand

Let's take a tiny 4-point discrete audio signal: $x = [1, 0, -1, 0]$ at sample times $n=0, 1, 2, 3$. Let's test for frequency $k=1$:

Step 1: Compute Basis Angles $\theta_n = 2\pi(1)n / 4 = n\pi/2$
For $n=0$: $\theta = 0 \implies e^{-i 0} = 1$
For $n=1$: $\theta = \pi/2 \implies e^{-i \pi/2} = -i$
For $n=2$: $\theta = \pi \implies e^{-i \pi} = -1$
For $n=3$: $\theta = 3\pi/2 \implies e^{-i 3\pi/2} = i$
Step 2: Multiply and Sum
$$X[1] = (1)(1) + (0)(-i) + (-1)(-1) + (0)(i) = 1 + 0 + 1 + 0 = 2$$
Step 3: Measure Frequency Magnitude
$$|X[1]| = \sqrt{2^2 + 0^2} = 2$$ Conclusion: The Fourier transform successfully extracted an amplitude of $2.0$ at fundamental frequency $k=1$!

🐍 4. Try It in Python (< 15 Lines)

Python 3 Runnable Snippet
import numpy as np

# Sample rate: 8000 Hz, duration 0.05 seconds
t = np.linspace(0, 0.05, 400, endpoint=False)
# 440 Hz (Concert A) + noise
signal = np.sin(2 * np.pi * 440 * t) + 0.4 * np.random.randn(len(t))

# Compute Fast Fourier Transform
fft_vals = np.fft.rfft(signal)
freqs = np.fft.rfftfreq(len(t), 1/8000)

peak_freq = freqs[np.argmax(np.abs(fft_vals))]
print(f"Detected strongest frequency: {peak_freq:.1f} Hz (Expected ~440 Hz)")
